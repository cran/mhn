Introduction to the mhn Package

The mhn package provides density, distribution, quantile, and random generation functions for the Modified Half-Normal (MHN) distribution, plus helpers for its moments and mode. This vignette walks through the basic usage of every exported function.

library(mhn)

1 The MHN distribution

The MHN(\(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\)) distribution has support on \((0, \infty)\) and density

\[f(x \mid \alpha, \beta, \gamma) = \frac{2 \beta^{\alpha/2} \, x^{\alpha-1} \, \exp(-\beta x^2 + \gamma x)} {\Psi[\alpha/2,\, \gamma/\sqrt{\beta}]}, \qquad x > 0,\]

where \(\alpha > 0\), \(\beta > 0\), \(\gamma \in \mathbb{R}\), and \(\Psi[a, z]\) is the Fox–Wright \({}_1\Psi_1\) function used as the normalising constant (Sun, Kong & Pal 2023). The three parameters control the polynomial factor \(x^{\alpha-1}\), the Gaussian tail \(\exp(-\beta x^2)\), and the exponential tilt \(\exp(\gamma x)\) respectively.

2 Density: dmhn()

dmhn() evaluates the density (or log-density) at one or more points:

dmhn(c(0.5, 1, 2), alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1)
#> [1] 0.4702986 0.7325378 0.1982764
dmhn(1, alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1, log = TRUE)
#> [1] -0.3112403

It is vectorised over both x and the parameters, with standard R recycling:

dmhn(c(0.5, 1, 1.5), alpha = c(1, 2), beta = 1, gamma = c(0, 1, -1))
#> [1] 0.87878258 0.73253783 0.04310111
x <- c(seq(0.001, 0.3, length.out = 60), seq(0.3, 5, length.out = 140))
plot(x, dmhn(x, alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1),
     type = "l", lwd = 2, ylab = "density", ylim = c(0, 1),
     main = expression("MHN(" * alpha * ", " * beta * ", " * gamma * ")"))
lines(x, dmhn(x, alpha = 0.5, beta = 1, gamma = 1), lwd = 2, col = "tomato")
lines(x, dmhn(x, alpha = 0.3, beta = 1, gamma = 4), lwd = 2, col = "steelblue")
legend("topright", bty = "n",
       legend = c("(2, 1, 1)    log-concave, mode near 1",
                  "(0.5, 1, 1)  monotone decreasing",
                  "(0.3, 1, 4)  boundary spike + interior bump"),
       col = c("black", "tomato", "steelblue"), lwd = 2)
MHN densities for three parameter triples; the steelblue curve sits in the alpha < 1, gamma >> 0 regime where the density combines a boundary divergence at x to 0+ with an interior local maximum near x = 1.8 (Sun et al. 2023, Lemma 3c). The y-axis is clipped at 1; the divergent left tails of the tomato and steelblue curves continue upward beyond the plot.

3 Distribution function: pmhn()

pmhn() returns \(P(X \le q)\) (or its complement / log-probability via lower.tail / log.p). In the general case each tail is computed directly on its own side of the peak of the density of \(\log X\), which lies at or above the median.

Below that peak the lower tail comes from one of two routes, chosen by measuring their cost and accuracy. One is the Sun et al. (2023) Lemma 1b series, summed in log space and truncated at an upper bound on the length \(K = \max\{K_1, K_2\}\) of their Supplementary Lemma 10(d), from the geometric decay of the terms that Lemma 10(a, b) establish. The other is a Boost.Math quadrature of the unnormalised density: Gauss–Kronrod, and for \(\alpha < 1\), where the density is unbounded at the origin, a closed form next to the origin, then tanh–sinh up to \(q\) or up to the interior peak that a positive tilt can give the density, whichever comes first, and Gauss–Kronrod past that peak. The series is tried first for \(\alpha < 1\) up to \(\gamma/\sqrt{\beta} = 10^3\), where the quadrature misses the working tolerance sqrt(.Machine$double.eps) at scattered points, and for \(\alpha \ge 1\) while \(|\gamma|/\sqrt{\beta} \le 20\) and \(\alpha \le 100\). Where it is tried, the quadrature still replaces it when, for \(\gamma < 0\), the cancellation between its alternating terms could push its error past that tolerance, when it would need more than \(10^6\) terms, and when \(\beta q^2\) is below the smallest normal double. Elsewhere the quadrature is used directly: there it is the faster of the two, and within the tolerance.

The upper tail \(P(X > q)\) is computed directly as well, by quadrature on a logarithmic axis, rather than as \(1 - F\), which would round to 0 once it falls below about \(10^{-16}\); above the peak, where it is the smaller tail, the lower tail is returned as one minus it. With log.p = TRUE both tails stay on the log scale, with two exceptions. At or below the median, where \(F \le 1/2\), \(\log P(X > q)\) is about \(-F\), and the logarithm of the quadrature carries an absolute error of about \(\epsilon\) times the size of the terms it is assembled from. Wherever that could pass \(10^{-9}\) of \(F\), below \(F\) of about \(10^{-5}\) for parameters of order one, \(\log P(X > q)\) is formed instead as \(\log(1 - F)\) from \(\log F\), which keeps the relative accuracy of a small \(F\). Likewise, below the peak of a small shape, where \(F\) is close to 1 and \(\log F\) is about \(-P(X > q)\), \(\log F\) is formed as \(\log(1 - P(X > q))\) from the upper tail wherever that is the more accurate; without that switch its error would pass \(10^{-9}\) for a shape below about \(10^{-6}\) under a small tilt. At a very large shape or tilt both tails are measured from the peak instead (see ?pmhn).

pmhn(c(0.5, 1, 1.5, 2), alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1)
#> [1] 0.1129101 0.4338796 0.7614259 0.9363038
pmhn(2, alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1, lower.tail = FALSE)
#> [1] 0.06369619
pmhn(2, alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1, log.p = TRUE)
#> [1] -0.06581527

A direct cross-check against integrate(dmhn, 0, q) confirms accuracy:

q <- 1.5
ref <- integrate(function(x) dmhn(x, 2, 1, 1), 0, q,
                 rel.tol = 1e-10)$value
all.equal(pmhn(q, 2, 1, 1), ref)
#> [1] TRUE
plot(x, dmhn(x, 2, 1, 1), type = "l", lwd = 2, ylab = "f(x)", main = "Density")
plot(x, pmhn(x, 2, 1, 1), type = "l", lwd = 2, ylab = "F(x)", main = "CDF",
     ylim = c(0, 1))
Density and matching CDF for MHN(2, 1, 1).

4 Quantile function: qmhn()

qmhn() inverts the CDF. Write \(p_\ell\) and \(p_u = 1 - p_\ell\) for the lower- and upper-tail probabilities: the argument p (its logarithm with log.p = TRUE) is \(p_\ell\) with lower.tail = TRUE and \(p_u\) with lower.tail = FALSE, and the other one is formed from it without cancellation where it is needed. In the general case a TOMS 748 root-finder solves \(F(x) = p_\ell\), or \(\log P(X > x) = \log p_u\) on the directly computed upper tail where that tail resolves the probability better: for an upper-tail p whose \(p_\ell\) that logarithm resolves to \(10^{-9}\) of itself (at least \(3 \times 10^{-6}\) or so from 1, more for a large shape, tilt or \(|\log\beta|\)), and for a lower-tail p close enough to 1 that \(F\) no longer resolves \(p_u\) to \(10^{-9}\) of itself (within about \(10^{-5}\) of 1 for parameters of order one, farther for a small or large shape, a large tilt or an extreme \(\beta\); see ?qmhn). Closer to 1 an upper-tail p is solved as \(F(x) = p_\ell\), or, where \(F\) comes from the series and costs far more, its upper-tail root is refined by Newton steps on \(\log F\); within \(10^{-7}\) of 1 that root is taken, where needed, for a \(p_\ell\) the upper tail still resolves. With log.p = TRUE, a lower-tail probability whose logarithm is below log(.Machine$double.xmin), about \(-708.4\), is solved as \(\log F(x) = \log p_\ell\) instead. The initial bracket is \([s\sqrt{\epsilon},\, s]\) with \(s = E(X) + 8 \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(X)}\) and \(\epsilon\) the machine epsilon; its left end is halved and its right end doubled until it brackets the target. Where the root-finder does not reach its tolerance on that bracket, as for a tiny lower-tail p at a large shape, it solves for \(\log x\) instead, on \(\log F\) for a lower-tail equation, and failing that the bracket is bisected. The special cases invert the laws they reduce to (see ?qmhn).

qmhn(c(0.1, 0.5, 0.9), alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1)
#> [1] 0.4717434 1.0906276 1.8480472

The round-trip identity holds within the inverter’s tolerance:

p <- c(0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 0.9, 0.99)
all.equal(pmhn(qmhn(p, 2, 1, 1), 2, 1, 1), p, tolerance = 1e-6)
#> [1] TRUE

lower.tail and log.p follow the same conventions as qnorm/qgamma:

qmhn(0.95, 2, 1, 1, lower.tail = FALSE)        # = qmhn(0.05)
#> [1] 0.3394014
qmhn(log(0.05), 2, 1, 1, log.p = TRUE)         # same value, log-input
#> [1] 0.3394014

5 Random generation: rmhn()

rmhn(n, alpha, beta, gamma) draws n variates. The default method = "auto" chooses between the special-case shortcuts (sqrt-Gamma for \(\gamma = 0\); truncated normal for \(\alpha = 1\)), Sun et al. (2023) Algorithms 1 / 3, and the Gao & Wang (2025) Relaxed Transformed Density Rejection (RTDR) sampler. method = "rtdr" or method = "sun" selects which of the two general-case samplers is used (with a few exceptions at the limits of double precision, listed in ?rmhn); the special-case shortcuts are taken first in either case, being exact and needing next to no setup. The default method chooses its sampler from the number of variates per setup, so under one seed its stream can change with n where that choice does (at (2, 1, 1) the first four draws of rmhn(100, ...) are not those of rmhn(4, ...)); method = "rtdr" or "sun" keeps one stream whatever n (see ?rmhn).

rmhn(5, alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1)
#> [1] 0.5497233 1.9097224 0.3042778 1.0601083 1.1484879

# Vector parameters are recycled to length n.
rmhn(5, alpha = c(1, 2, 3, 4, 5))
#> [1] 0.5649849 0.1332783 1.1693196 1.3508566 0.9682530
set.seed(42)
draws <- rmhn(10000, alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 1)
hist(draws, breaks = 60, probability = TRUE, col = "grey90", border = "white",
     xlab = "x", main = "rmhn(10000, 2, 1, 1)")
lines(x, dmhn(x, 2, 1, 1), lwd = 2, col = "tomato")
10,000 draws from MHN(2, 1, 1) with the true density overlaid.

Switching method is useful for benchmarking. Both samplers draw the exact law, so a sample from either is consistent with it; here two samples, drawn one after the other from a single seed, are each compared with the distribution function by a one-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov test:

set.seed(1)
s_rtdr <- rmhn(5000, 2, 1, 1, method = "rtdr")
s_sun  <- rmhn(5000, 2, 1, 1, method = "sun")
F <- function(q) pmhn(q, 2, 1, 1)
c(rtdr = ks.test(s_rtdr, F)$p.value, sun = ks.test(s_sun, F)$p.value)
#>      rtdr       sun 
#> 0.3785647 0.6279249

6 Moments and mode

The package provides helpers for the common summary statistics. The mean is the Fox–Wright ratio of Sun et al. (2023, Lemma 2a) and the variance follows from it by their Lemma 2c wherever these closed forms are accurate; elsewhere, and for the skewness and kurtosis, the moments are integrated numerically against the density. The mode is the closed form of Sun et al. (2023, Lemma 3).

data.frame(
  Quantity = c("mean", "variance", "skewness", "excess kurtosis", "mode"),
  Function = c("mhn_mean", "mhn_var", "mhn_skewness",
               "mhn_kurtosis", "mhn_mode"),
  Value = c(
    mhn_mean(2, 1, 1),
    mhn_var(2, 1, 1),
    mhn_skewness(2, 1, 1),
    mhn_kurtosis(2, 1, 1),
    mhn_mode(2, 1, 1)
  )
)
#>          Quantity     Function       Value
#> 1            mean     mhn_mean 1.133731086
#> 2        variance      mhn_var 0.281519367
#> 3        skewness mhn_skewness 0.463887428
#> 4 excess kurtosis mhn_kurtosis 0.006868473
#> 5            mode     mhn_mode 1.000000000

For \(\alpha < 1\) without an interior local maximum (for instance with \(\gamma \le 0\)), mhn_mode() returns NA; at \(\alpha = 1\) with \(\gamma \le 0\) it returns the boundary mode 0:

mhn_mode(0.5, 1, -1)
#> [1] NA
mhn_mode(1, 1, -1)
#> [1] 0

7 Special cases

The MHN family contains several familiar distributions:

Constraint Reduction
\(\gamma = 0\) \(X^2 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha/2, \beta)\), rate \(\beta\) (sqrt-Gamma)
\(\alpha = 1\) Truncated normal on \((0, \infty)\)
\(\alpha = 1, \gamma = 0\) Half-normal \(|Z|, Z \sim N(0, 1/(2\beta))\)

At \(\gamma = 0\) and at \(\alpha = 1\) exactly, dmhn / pmhn / qmhn work through the corresponding closed form, the Gamma law of \(\beta X^2\) or the truncated normal, rather than the general series; a shape or a tilt merely close to those values is a different law, and goes through the general route, which keeps its accuracy there. rmhn draws from the closed forms also for \(|\alpha - 1|\) or \(|\gamma|/\sqrt{\beta}\) below sqrt(.Machine$double.eps), a law within a total variation distance of \(8.6 \times 10^{-9}\) of the one requested. A zero tilt goes through the general route above a shape of \(10^6\) (\(2^{60}\) for rmhn), where the Gamma law is too narrow for the rounding of \(\beta x^2\) or of rgamma.

# gamma = 0: dmhn matches the change-of-variable sqrt-Gamma density.
xx <- c(0.5, 1, 1.5, 2)
mhn_d  <- dmhn(xx, alpha = 2, beta = 1, gamma = 0)
ref_d  <- dgamma(xx^2, shape = 1, rate = 1) * 2 * xx
all.equal(mhn_d, ref_d)
#> [1] TRUE
mu_tn    <- 1 / (2 * 1)
sigma_tn <- 1 / sqrt(2 * 1)
tn_dens  <- function(x) {
  dnorm(x, mu_tn, sigma_tn) / pnorm(0, mu_tn, sigma_tn, lower.tail = FALSE)
}
plot(x, dmhn(x, 1, 1, 1), type = "l", lwd = 2, ylab = "density",
     main = "alpha = 1: MHN reduces to truncated normal")
points(x, tn_dens(x), pch = ".", col = "tomato", cex = 2)
legend("topright", bty = "n",
       legend = c("dmhn(x, 1, 1, 1)", "TN reference"),
       col = c("black", "tomato"), lwd = c(2, NA), pch = c(NA, 19))
MHN(1, 1, 1) and the equivalent truncated normal density.

8 Further reading

  • See ?dmhn, ?pmhn, ?qmhn, ?rmhn for full argument documentation, including the recycling rules and the method argument of rmhn.
  • citation("mhn") returns four entries: the package itself and the three underlying papers.
  • Sun, Kong & Pal (2023) develop the parametric family and Algorithms 1 and 3 used here. Gao & Wang (2025) introduce the RTDR sampler, whose acceptance they bound below by \(1/e\) where its contact points lie in a band they give.
